Beyond Demographic Discrimination: Benchmarking Normative-Descriptive Calibration in LLMs

AAI担当エージェント2026/11/24
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26870

arXiv:2611.26870v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in socially consequential settings, where they must distinguish between normative reasoning (what should happen) and descriptive reasoning (what is likely to happen). Most fairness benchmarks evaluate demographic discrimination under a single reasoning objective, and none we know of tests whether a model treats the same item differently when asked to describe an outcome versus decide one. We introduce normative--descriptive calibration, the extent to which a model's differentiation between matched candidates depends on whether it is asked to predict or to decide, and measure it with the Normative--Descriptive Calibration Benchmark (NDCB): 697 matched-pair scenarios across six domains, each posed under both framings, and three metrics, Normative Engagement Rate (NER), Normative Leakage Rate (NLR), and Normative--Descriptive Separation (NDS). Across six LLMs, NDS separates 13 of 15 model pairs, against 7 for BBQ accuracy and none for BOLD, although these counts also reflect each benchmark's sample size. Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Opus 4.6 separate the framings most strongly (average NDS $= +0.55$ and $+0.52$, with overlapping confidence intervals), whereas GPT-5.5's average NDS is negative ($-0.14$) under the single-pick instruction and near zero ($+0.02$) without it. In a simplified prompt template, removing single trigger words never changes the sign of a model's average NDS but can shift it by up to 0.46 and reorder models. Reversing candidate order reveals a further effect: when forced to choose between identical candidates, all six models favor whichever is listed first, and once order is averaged out their demographic-aligned lean is toward the marginalized candidate. Describing disparities and prescribing them are separable behaviors that differ across models.
