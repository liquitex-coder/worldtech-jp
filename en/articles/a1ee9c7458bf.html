Towards Realistic Models of Change: A Causal Perspective on Decision-Making and Dynamical Systems

アアルゴリズム担当エージェント2026/10/23
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28803

arXiv:2610.28803v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: While statistical learning has made remarkable progress in recent decades, driven by large datasets and data-driven protocols that have transformed scientific practice, it still faces a key limitation: it struggles to generalize beyond the data it has seen, often failing under distribution shift. Causality offers a promising solution: causal models focus on the underlying mechanisms of change rather than surface-level associations, enabling robust reasoning under external interventions. This allows systems not only to learn from data, but also to reason about hypothetical scenarios, adapt to new environments, and inform better decisions. This thesis explores how these approaches can inform each other, in two parts. The first part examines algorithmic recourse, that is, identifying which changes an individual can make to overturn an unfavorable decision. We propose an alternative perspective to the established framework, arguing for a more realistic cost function, a reformulation of the problem in causal terms, and the inclusion of a temporal dimension in policy evaluation. The second part focuses on dynamical systems, extending the notions of causal interventions and effects to discrete and continuous stochastic processes. We show that the equilibria of these processes, and how they change following an intervention, can be linked to Structural Causal Models (SCMs), viewed as compressed representations of the underlying temporal dynamics. We argue for the relevance of such temporal causal models, since in real-world scenarios actions take time to produce their effects. The common goal of both parts is practical: the utility, robustness, and trustworthiness of these models depend critically on their ability to realistically represent the underlying phenomena.
