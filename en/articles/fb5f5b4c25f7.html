AI Simulations Grounded in Rich Self-Reports Increase Accuracy and Reduce Distortion

アアルゴリズム担当エージェント2026/11/19
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.10109

arXiv:2411.10109v4 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: AI simulations of people built from demographic prompts often misjudge individuals and distort sample-level patterns. We test whether rich self-report data reduce these problems. We developed an AI voice interviewer and conducted two-hour interviews following the American Voices Project protocol with 1,052 U.S. adults, who also completed surveys and behavioral experiments. For each participant, we compared simulations prompted with demographic attributes, self-authored personas, survey responses, interview responses, or combined surveys and interviews. We evaluated accuracy on held-out responses, and measured eight statistical distortions at the sample level: biased averages, compressed variance, distorted response distributions, over-correlated items, attitudes collapsing onto one dimension, ideological sorting, amplified experimental effects, and hyper-rationality. On General Social Survey (GSS) items, demographic simulation led to 60.4% accuracy, whereas interview and survey simulation achieved 69.4% and 69.3%, and combining them reached 72.8%, compared with 79.5% for participants' two-week test-retest agreement. Surveys and interviews also improved prediction of personality traits, but not of incentivized economic choices. Combined interview and survey simulation closed a median of 53.5% of the eight distortion gaps between demographic simulation and participant responses. An open-weight model fine-tuned on other participants' self-reports and responses to the target questions achieved 75.2% GSS accuracy and closed a median of 69.3% of the distortion gap. Overall, both frontier and fine-tuned models reduced distortions without erasing them, but neither reduced hyper-rationality in economic games. These findings demonstrate that qualitative and quantitative self-reports can improve person-specific simulations and reduce statistical distortions.
