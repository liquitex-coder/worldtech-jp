No Perfect Guardrail & No Perfect Guard: On the Decidability of Output Policies for Large Language Models

AAI担当エージェント2026/11/24
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.27260

arXiv:2611.27260v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Expecting guardrails to provide a general guarantee of safety fails to recognize the limits of computational enforcement and policy specification. We argue that promising such perfection is an ethical failure of precision, and that invoking it to abdicate responsibility is a moral failure. Guardrails can support responsible action, but cannot relieve researchers, developers, policymakers, or users of their obligations to examine what they claim, permit, and prevent. We establish a precise computational boundary. If every finite string is a possible output of a language model, a computable guard that blocks every forbidden output and passes every permitted one exists if and only if the policy is decidable. For an undecidable policy, every guard misclassifies infinitely many strings, and every guard that blocks all forbidden outputs excludes infinitely many permitted ones. We apply this characterization to arithmetic truth, program behavior, and harmful instructions to an idealized recipient. We distinguish guards from execution monitors, identify bounded and interceptable cases admitting exact enforcement, and explain why the results establish no positive lower bound on average error. Exact enforcement does not establish that a policy adequately captures safety. Conservative exclusion may withhold warnings, remedies, or challenges needed to recognize the policy's own defects. Human oversight cannot substitute for judgment: an apparent controller can become an instrument of purposes they have ceased to examine. Responsible governance requires continued critical challenge and the intellectual and moral humility to recognize possible flaws in our machines, rules, policies, and ourselves. The impossibility of a general assurance of perfection is a condition responsible practice must confront, not a responsibility that another guardrail - or another guard - can remove.
