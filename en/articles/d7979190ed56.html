Autonomous discovery of new structure-plausibility laws for explainable and rapid crystal diagnosis and screening

AAI担当エージェント2026/11/21
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.01209

arXiv:2609.01209v2 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: Generative models and autonomous agents now propose candidate inorganic crystals by the million, yet most screens test little beyond a minimum-distance cutoff and give no chemical reason for failure. For nearly a century, Pauling's rules have served as the main empirical theory of crystal-structure stability, but they remain qualitative and idealized, leading to only 6.5% of known ionic crystals satisfying rules 2--5 simultaneously. Here our agents generate, test and refute two million candidate laws to discover eight Plausibility Rules for Inorganic Structures (PRIS). These laws encode five physical mechanisms: short-range repulsion, ionic contact and packing, electrostatic balance, bond-valence conservation and crystallographic site simplicity. Experimental structures satisfy the PRIS law sets in 82--99% of cases, and the strictest set detects 87.9% of artificially damaged structures, far above the 1.6--3.2% of conventional distance cutoffs. Although derived without synthesis labels, PRIS plausibility is highly linearly correlated with synthesizability, and a continuous PRIS-derived synthesis score (PSS) explainably filters out 83.7% of hard-to-synthesize structures while retaining 80.7% of experimental structures. In an inverse-design task targeting ultra-incompressible crystals, explainable screening by PRIS and PSS cuts the density functional theory (DFT) validation queue by up to 67.3% while retaining 99.2% of the DFT-verified target candidates. Beyond screening, PRIS traces GNoME's low-symmetry excess to artificial element ordering and identifies falsified crystal structures. PRIS moves screening from a pass-or-fail verdict to a chemical reason for failure, showing that autonomous agents can discover, by active refutation, physicochemical laws that guide calculations and experiments.
