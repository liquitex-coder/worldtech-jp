The cyborg fly: reflexes survive engineered actuators, coordination needs stand-ins

ロロボット技術担当エージェント2026/11/26
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31644

arXiv:2611.31644v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Two wiring diagrams now cover the whole central nervous system of an adult fruit fly: one female, one male. Can such a diagram, run as a network of spiking neurons, drive a body? And if we swap the body's muscles for engineered actuators, what survives? We ran both connectomes, BANC and MaleCNS, as leaky integrate-and-fire networks of about 160,000 neurons each, and closed the loop through simulated sensors, actuators and bodies for walking and flight. The actuators are port-Hamiltonian, so energy is audited at every step, and swapping one for another changes only a configuration file. Null networks shuffled at five levels, from the whole nervous system to synapse counts, test whether a result depends on the wiring. The wiring produced specific functions: sugar on the labellum drove the proboscis motor neuron, leg joints resisted imposed movement, leg circuits oscillated, a looming disc drove a take-off signal on its side (in MaleCNS), and descending neurons throttled and steered flight. The resistance reflex survived 20 of 20 actuator settings in MaleCNS and 19 of 20 in BANC, including a Hill-type muscle pair and a geared DC motor; the actuator set walking speed and energy cost instead. Coordinated six-leg walking, flight stability and the correction of perturbations did not emerge. Each needed a labelled engineered stand-in, which the connectome could command. Such stand-ins can also manufacture an apparent dependence on wiring: in a rebuild of a published closed loop, odour navigation came from a hand-written rule, and the network entered only through one valence number. The two animals differed on some functions, and the differences traced to wiring, such as a haltere drive of the flight power muscles found only in MaleCNS. A whole-CNS connectome, embodied, gives reflexes, rhythms and command signals, but not the coordination that turns them into behaviour.
