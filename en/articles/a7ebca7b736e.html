Structured Kernel Surrogate Models for Multimodal Attribution

アアルゴリズム担当エージェント2026/11/12
ENOriginal-language text (excerpt). 日本語訳は こちら。 Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13198

arXiv:2611.13198v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: We study the problem of cross-sample modality attribution, which involves measuring how one modality in one sample affects the learning outcome in other multimodal samples. This problem is crucial in a multimodal system because a data modality (such as a text description) may be redundant for one sample (a self-explanatory image), but the same data modality would otherwise be uniquely informative for another sample, or informative when combined with other modalities. Prior work focuses on influence estimation at the level of one sample, and it remains an open question how one should capture the interactions across both samples and modalities. To capture scenarios like the above, we propose structured kernel surrogate models, which are surrogate mappings from both samples and modalities to the prediction outcome when they are trained together. We design an algorithm, SKS, which applies kernel ridge regression with a structured, compositional kernel on the selection states of individual samples and modalities. Specifically, the kernel function multiplies kernel-based similarity scores between every pair of data modalities for one sample and a pair of two samples. Additionally, we design a gradient-based estimation algorithm to efficiently estimate the structured kernel surrogate model. We evaluate our approach on two multimodal benchmarks, spanning a range of data modalities including satellite images, road networks, text, audio, and tabular data. We find that SKS achieves $57.8$% higher correlation with the true prediction outcomes than existing methods. In downstream data selection, SKS improves the average accuracy by $1.93$% over baselines. Models with data selected by SKS have lower Hessian traces, indicating a flatter loss surface. Using SKS to select satellite images identifies road segments, where accident scenes commonly occur, as opposed to background images.
