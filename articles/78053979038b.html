ORIGINAL · ENarXiv:2611.21001v2 Announce Type: replace-cross Abstract: Autonomous voice agents now conduct routine clinical follow-up calls, so some automated triage is unavoidable. A patient becoming frustrated is one thing that triage should catch. These systems typically read only the transcript the speech-to-text stage produces, not the voice, and whether that is enough is largely untested on real clinical speech. We study per-turn frustration detection on real patient calls from a deployed clinical voice agent: 150 turns from six calls labelled by three annotators, with a length-matched acted corpus as a control, and 714 turns from 30 calls. Patient turns are overwhelmingly short: across the deployed calls, roughly 59% are one or two words, against 13% in the full acted corpus, and a corpus accuracy gap remains even after the acted set is length-matched. On one-word turns, transcript-only detection does not exclude chance. Across five audio-capable language models, the transcript flags 3 or 4 of the 39 frustrated one-word turns in the 30 calls, and a classifier given only the word count reaches 57.3%. Whether audio helps depends on the model and on the instruction. Asked to attend to delivery, two Gemini 2.5 models gain 3.0 pp over the transcript across 30 calls (-1.0 to 7.5 pp). Of the turns they confidently clear, 6.9% were in fact frustrated for audio against 13.0% for the transcript, a difference of 6.1 pp (2.2 to 10.4 pp). Across the 30 calls, given one prompt identical for both inputs, no model's audio-text interval excludes zero. Under the input-specific prompts, a newer Gemini model and two smaller open models do worse with audio than with the transcript (-3.5, -8.6 and -10.6 pp). A WavLM probe trained on the clinical calls matches the best prompted model overall (71.6%) but misses every frustrated one-word turn in the 24 added calls. When a patient's turn is short, the transcript alone is not enough to rule out frustration.
