ORIGINAL · ENarXiv:2611.27540v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Large robot teams are often run as a plan-then-execute stack. We study a decentralized instance for drone swarms, a mean-field-game (MFG) routing field with a per-agent control-barrier-function (CBF) safety filter, when communication is limited by a shared TDMA channel. Neighbors are known only through noisy range and bearing measurements exchanged in round-robin slots, so the frame, and with it the age of every estimate, grows with swarm size. In simulation this delay reaches both layers but breaks only the safety layer. Without delay, swarms of up to 100 agents do not collide, even with each measurement's noise held for a whole frame; with it, stale estimates at the CBF cause collisions beyond a frame period of 125 to 175 ms, or 75 to 100 ms at 100 agents with held noise, and swarm size sets where that limit lies and how steeply collisions rise. At the MFG, staleness makes the local density noisier without biasing it, and throughput drops by at most a tenth. We then compensate with lightweight on-board predictors, from constant-velocity fits to a small learned network, that estimate each neighbor's position from its last broadcasts. A layer-split experiment shows where prediction is needed: at the CBF it restores safety and extends the tolerated frame period by 1.4 to 1.7 times with an untrained constant-velocity fit and about 2.3 with the learned network when the noise is redrawn each step; with noise held for a frame the two extend it about equally, while at the MFG it only refines the congestion correction. A constant wider safety margin, the simplest alternative, makes collisions more frequent in a bottleneck. With the routing solved offline, that layer is robust to the delay because it sees neighbors only through a crowd average; compensation belongs in the safety filter, switched on only above the uncompensated limit, since below it the predictor's own error adds collisions.
